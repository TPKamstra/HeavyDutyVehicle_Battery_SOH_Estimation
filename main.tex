\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

Heavy-duty diesel vehicles rely on a starter battery capable of delivering several hundred amperes for a short but critical interval each time the engine is started. Compared to light-duty passenger vehicles, these platforms typically combine larger-displacement, higher-compression diesel engines with harsher duty cycles: long idle periods interspersed with mission-critical starts, wide ambient temperature ranges, and vibration and shock loads that accelerate mechanical wear inside the battery. A failed or marginal start is not merely an inconvenience in this context --- for fleet operators, emergency services, and other applications where the vehicle must be available on demand, an unexpected failure to crank can take an entire asset out of service at the worst possible moment. \todo{if the paper is meant to stay anchored to the armoured-vehicle motivating case in the introduction, add one sentence here noting that armoured/military heavy-duty vehicles are a representative and demanding example of this vehicle class, without going into vehicle-specific detail} The consequences of an undetected failure are more severe still when the vehicle is deployed away from base --- on a mission, at a remote worksite, or otherwise out in the field --- where a spare battery, a replacement, or even diagnostic equipment may not be readily available; a failure that would be a minor, scheduled task in a depot becomes an operational disruption in the field. This asymmetry --- low cost of replacing a battery proactively versus high cost of a failure at the wrong time and place --- is precisely what makes early, reliable SOH estimation valuable for this vehicle class.

A further practical complication, and one that shapes the methodology of this paper, is the absence of ground-truth SOH labels for batteries in field operation. Laboratory aging campaigns can define SOH unambiguously because the battery's full history and a reference measurement (e.g., a capacity or CCA test) are available on demand. In the field, however, batteries are not routinely pulled from vehicles and capacity-tested; the only readily available signal is whatever can be observed non-invasively via the vehicle's own instrumentation, primarily its CAN bus. Consequently, this paper's field dataset has no directly measured SOH label to regress against, and the lab-to-field comparison in Section~\ref{sec:validation} is necessarily framed in terms of whether \emph{feature behavior} (trends, distributions, SoC-dependence) is consistent between the lab and the field, rather than in terms of absolute field SOH accuracy. No proxy label (e.g. workshop replacement records or CCA test logs) is currently available for the field vehicles either, so this limitation is restated explicitly in Section~\ref{sec:discussion} and should inform how the Results in Section~\ref{sec:results} are interpreted: agreement between lab and field should be read as evidence of consistent \emph{feature behavior}, not as validated field SOH accuracy.

State-of-health (SOH) estimation aims to quantify the remaining capability of a battery --- typically expressed relative to its capacity or cranking-current capability when new --- so that a degrading battery can be flagged and replaced before it fails to start the vehicle. For starter batteries specifically, SOH is commonly inferred from the voltage response during a cranking event: the depth of the voltage dip when the starter motor draws current, the shape of the voltage recovery afterward, and derived quantities such as internal resistance all change measurably as the battery ages~\cite{kerleyAutomotiveLeadacidBattery2015,stepresponse_soh}. These cranking-voltage-based methods are attractive because they require no additional current sensor and can, in principle, be implemented using signals already available on the vehicle's data bus.

However, most published SOH estimation methods for lead-acid and AGM starter batteries are developed and validated under controlled laboratory conditions: a single battery (or small batch) is aged on a bench under a scripted, repeatable protocol, and the resulting features are correlated against a reference SOH measurement obtained under the same controlled conditions~\cite{lead_acid_impedance_aging,eis_soh_timedep,ruetschi_aging}. Laboratory aging protocols are valuable because they allow SOH to be tracked against a trustworthy ground truth and because operating conditions --- temperature, load profile, SoC at the time of cranking --- can be swept systematically. What they cannot fully capture is the variability of real-world use: field batteries experience unscripted combinations of temperature, partial charge history, and load that are difficult to reproduce exactly on a bench. Recent work on electric-vehicle traction batteries has shown that this laboratory-to-field gap is real and non-trivial: models and features that perform well on laboratory cycling data can degrade substantially when applied to field data, because field data is noisier, less regularly sampled, and subject to a much wider range of operating conditions than laboratory data~\cite{labfield_gap,transferable_capacity_estimation,multimodal_ev_field}. To date, this transferability question has been studied primarily for lithium-ion traction batteries in electric vehicles. Whether the same gap exists --- and how large it is --- for lead-acid/AGM \emph{starter} batteries under high-current cranking loads in heavy-duty diesel vehicles is, to the best of our knowledge, not well documented in the literature.

This paper addresses that gap directly. We combine two complementary data sources:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A controlled laboratory aging campaign.} A battery of the same type used in the target vehicle class is degraded on an automated test bench (built around a Raspberry Pi controller) through ten realistic test days, each exercising the battery at a different operating state of charge. Cranking-event and cycling data recorded during these test days are used to derive a set of candidate SOH-indicating features together with a reference SOH trajectory for the battery under test.
\item \textbf{Field CAN-bus data from operational vehicles.} Cranking-event data recorded via the vehicle CAN bus during real-world engine starts on in-service heavy-duty diesel vehicles is used as an independent field dataset, against which the laboratory-derived features are re-evaluated.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Cranking-Voltage and Impedance-Based SOH Estimation}

A substantial body of work estimates lead-acid/AGM battery SOH from the voltage signature recorded during an engine-cranking event; see~\cite{jiangReviewStateHealth2022} for a review specific to lead-acid SOH estimation methods, and~\cite{nouraReviewBatteryState2020,kimBatteryStateHealth2025} for reviews of the broader SOH estimation landscape. Kerley, Hyun, and Ha propose a SOH monitoring system for lead-acid car batteries based on the cranking voltage waveform, extracting critical voltage points from the response and relating them to battery health without requiring a dedicated current sensor~\cite{kerleyAutomotiveLeadacidBattery2015}. A parity-relation-based approach has also been proposed for automotive battery SOH monitoring, comparing the observed charging excitation voltage against a model-predicted value to flag degradation~\cite{zhangAutomotiveBatteryStateofhealth2012}. A complementary line of work uses a step-response model together with in-situ vehicle data to estimate SOH from the battery's dynamic response to a load step, which is conceptually close to a cranking event but framed in terms of an identified equivalent-circuit model rather than raw voltage features~\cite{stepresponse_soh}; dynamic lead-acid battery models built directly from manufacturer discharge-curve data offer a related, lower-effort route to a similar equivalent-circuit representation~\cite{medoraDynamicBatteryModeling2005}.

Impedance-based methods form a second major family. AC impedance (commonly measured near 1~kHz) increases as a lead-acid battery ages due to sulfation, active-material degradation, and grid corrosion, and has been used both as a direct SOH indicator and as a way to validate voltage- or current-based features against an independent physical measurement~\cite{lead_acid_impedance_aging,eis_soh_timedep,tairovImpedanceMeasurementsBattery2011}. For lead-acid chemistries specifically, EIS has also been used to assess SOH in the context of second-life applications~\cite{mohsinNewLeadacidBattery2022}. Some laboratory protocols combine impedance measurements taken at two distinct states of charge (e.g., full charge and 75\% SoC) to predict remaining battery lifetime with good accuracy~\cite{eis_soh_timedep}, which is directly relevant to the SoC-sweeping design used in this paper's laboratory campaign (Section~\ref{sec:method}).

\subsection{Lead-Acid Battery Aging Mechanisms and SoC Dependence}

The dominant aging mechanisms of lead-acid batteries --- grid and post corrosion, degradation and loss of adherence of the positive active mass, irreversible sulfation, internal short circuits, and water loss --- are well characterized in the foundational review by Ruetschi~\cite{ruetschi_aging}. Of particular relevance to this work, several of these mechanisms are strongly state-of-charge dependent: increasing depth-of-discharge during cycling accelerates positive active-mass degradation, and cumulative time spent at low SoC (commonly cited around SoC~$<$~35\%) is itself an aging stress factor, independent of the number of cycles performed~\cite{ruetschi_aging,lead_acid_impedance_aging}. This motivates deliberately sampling multiple SoC operating points --- rather than aging the battery at a single fixed SoC --- when designing a laboratory campaign intended to produce features that remain valid across the SoC range a starter battery actually experiences in the field. Beyond electrochemical stressors, heavy-duty and off-road duty cycles also subject the battery to sustained vibration and shock loading, separately shown to accelerate lead-acid degradation through plate and grid damage~\cite{heVibrationTestMethods2014} --- a further reason to age the battery under a realistic, vehicle-representative test-day protocol rather than a purely electrical bench cycle. Hybrid lead-acid/lithium-ion energy-storage architectures, in which a smaller lithium-ion buffer absorbs high-current transients to reduce cycling stress on the lead-acid unit, have also been proposed for transport applications~\cite{zauReviewBatteryManagement2020} and illustrate one mitigation strategy for the aging mechanisms considered here.

\subsection{Field-Data-Driven and Machine-Learning SOH Estimation}

Outside the lead-acid/starter-battery domain, a large and fast-growing literature addresses SOH estimation for lithium-ion traction batteries using field data collected from electric vehicles, typically via onboard telematics or CAN-bus logging. Health indicators are extracted from charging-phase voltage/current/temperature time series and fed to models ranging from Gaussian process regression to CNNs, RNNs, and LSTMs~\cite{multimodal_ev_field,domain_ml_soh}. Recent studies report that models trained on a single fleet or dataset generalize poorly to other vehicles or fleets, motivating transfer-learning and domain-adaptation approaches that explicitly try to bridge differences between training and deployment distributions~\cite{robust_soh_buses,transferable_capacity_estimation}.

Most relevant to the present paper is a recent and still-limited strand of work that studies the laboratory-to-field gap explicitly, rather than working with laboratory or field data in isolation. This work argues that laboratory cycling protocols use predefined, temperature-controlled conditions that do not reflect the partial charging/discharging, variable load, and calendar-aging effects seen in real-world operation, and that SOH algorithms tuned on laboratory data can consequently underperform when applied to field data~\cite{labfield_gap,transferable_capacity_estimation}. These studies are, however, concentrated on lithium-ion EV traction batteries. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has directly evaluated whether cranking-based SOH features derived from a controlled laboratory aging campaign transfer to field CAN-bus data recorded on high-current starter batteries in heavy-duty diesel vehicles. This is the gap addressed in the present paper.

\subsection{Battery Monitoring in Heavy-Duty and Military Vehicle Contexts}

Reliability of the energy supply system, including the starter battery, is also studied directly in the military vehicle literature: Kocsis Szürke proposes a hierarchical diagnostics and risk-assessment procedure for military vehicle batteries based on rapid pre-deployment testing, motivated by the fact that an unexpected power deficit is not only costly but can endanger personnel~\cite{military_energies2022}. This work targets pre-deployment risk scoring rather than a lab-to-field feature-transferability study, and does not address high-current starter batteries specifically, but it confirms that battery-driven mission risk is an established concern in this vehicle class and that rapid, field-deployable diagnostics (as opposed to full laboratory characterization) are the practical constraint operators work under. More broadly, predictive maintenance of heavy-duty vehicle components is an active research area in its own right; recent component-level prognostics work on heavy-duty trucks~\cite{dimidovEmpiricalStudyPredictive2025} and general treatments of the practical challenges of deploying predictive maintenance in the field~\cite{tingaPracticalApplicationPredictive2025} both underscore that translating lab- or fleet-analytics-derived indicators into reliable in-service monitoring remains difficult even outside the battery domain specifically.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}
% ============================================================

The methodology consists of two stages that share a common feature-extraction pipeline: (1) a controlled laboratory aging campaign that induces realistic degradation in a representative battery and produces a labeled feature/SOH dataset, and (2) a field-validation stage in which the same features are extracted from CAN-bus data recorded on operational heavy-duty diesel vehicles and compared against the laboratory findings. \subsection{Battery Under Test}
\label{sec:battery}

The laboratory campaign uses 12~V, 18~Ah valve-regulated lead-acid (VRLA/AGM) batteries (six 2~V cells in series) of the same nominal capacity class as starter batteries used in the target heavy-duty vehicle class. Per the manufacturer datasheet, the unit under test is rated for 15.3~Ah at the C/5 rate to a 1.75~V/cell cutoff, has a new-condition AC internal resistance of approximately 16~m$\Omega$, can sustain 270~A discharge for 5~s, and is specified for cycle-use charging at 14.4--15.0~V (max. 5.4~A) with an end-of-discharge cutoff of 10.8~V (1.8~V/cell) at the standard rate. Two physical units are tracked through the campaign under distinct identifiers: one unit that entered the campaign already substantially aged (delivering only $\sim$8~Ah of its 18~Ah nameplate capacity at the start of logging, since retired) and a second, fresh unit whose full degradation history from new is captured by the campaign described below. \todo{state explicitly whether Results/Discussion in this draft use one unit, the other, or both, and whether the two are pooled or reported separately given their different starting condition.} Using a production battery of the vehicle's own capacity/voltage class, rather than a generic reference cell, is intended to keep the cranking-current demand, internal resistance, and aging behavior representative of what is actually observed on the vehicle's own instrumentation during field validation (Section~\ref{sec:fielddata}).

\subsection{Laboratory Test Bench}
\label{sec:testbench}

The test bench is built around a Raspberry Pi running a Python-based orchestration layer that sequences charge, discharge, degradation-cycling, and test-day-profile phases and exposes a live web dashboard for monitoring and control. Electrical loading is applied via a bidirectional programmable power supply, addressed over a TCP/SCPI interface, capable of sourcing (charging) or sinking (discharging) up to 90~A / 15~V / 1500~W; this replaces a fixed resistive load or relay-switched load bank with a supply that can execute an arbitrary current or voltage profile in either polarity, which is what allows the event-scripted test-day profile of Section~\ref{sec:testdays} to be replayed directly rather than approximated. Voltage and current are logged by the power supply itself at 1~Hz during charge/discharge/degradation phases and at 20~Hz during test-day-profile replay (the higher rate being needed to resolve the sub-second structure of a simulated cranking pulse). An independent sensor board (microcontroller-based, serial link to the Pi) samples battery terminal voltage (via a voltage divider) and current (via a Hall-effect current sensor) at 20~Hz, together with ambient temperature and humidity at 1~Hz, providing a cross-check against the power supply's own measurement channel. All logs are timestamped in a common per-battery, per-run naming scheme and written to persistent storage for offline processing by the analytics pipeline described in Section~\ref{sec:features}.

\subsection{Design of the SoC-Sweeping Test-Day Protocol}
\label{sec:testdays}

Rather than aging the battery under a single fixed condition, the campaign repeatedly executes a realistic test-day load profile at up to ten different states of charge per degradation block, sweeping from fully charged down toward the discharge cutoff. This design is motivated by the SoC-dependent aging mechanisms discussed in Section~\ref{sec:related}: because both the electrochemical aging rate and the electrical behavior of the battery during a cranking event depend on SoC, features that are only characterized at a single SoC risk failing to generalize across the SoC range the battery actually experiences in the field.

A key design decision is how SoC is set and labeled. A fixed Ah step-down (e.g., discharge a constant 10\% of nameplate capacity between sweep points) becomes a meaningless SoC label as the battery degrades, since a fixed Ah quantity is a shrinking, then eventually undefined, fraction of a capacity that is itself falling. Instead, each sweep point is defined and verified by open-circuit voltage (OCV) after a rest period, using a piecewise-linear OCV--SoC lookup table calibrated for this VRLA/AGM chemistry at 25\textdegree{}C (Table~\ref{tab:testdays}). This choice is deliberate: it matches the only SoC-related quantity that is realistically observable on the vehicle at rest (terminal voltage), so the lab-side SoC label and the field-side SoC proxy are defined the same way, rather than the lab relying on a Coulomb-counted SoC that has no field equivalent. At each sweep point, the battery is stepped down using a rest-first loop --- disable load, rest, measure OCV, and only discharge further if OCV is still above the next target --- so that a battery that already reached a target point (e.g., as a side effect of the previous test-day profile) is not discharged unnecessarily.

At each sweep point, a single ``realistic test day'' is executed as an explicit, named event script rather than an unstructured load: an open-circuit rest window, a small wake-up/parasitic load, a glow-plug-like preheat load, a first (``cold'') high-current crank pulse, a recovery rest, a second glow-plug-like load, a second (``hot'') crank pulse, a further recovery rest, and finally an alternator-like charge phase followed by a variable driving/auxiliary-load phase. Including two crank events per test day, rather than one, allows the same battery's cranking signature to be compared across a cold-start-like and a warm-restart-like condition at the same SoC, in addition to across SoC. \todo{state the actual crank-pulse current/duration values used, and the wake-up/glow-plug/driving-load current levels, from the profile CSV, so a reader can judge how demanding the simulated crank is relative to the datasheet's 270~A/5~s rating.} Ten deep degradation cycles (discharge and CC/CV recharge at the C/2 rate) are interleaved between successive sweeps to progressively age the battery, and a full C/5-rate discharge to the 10.8~V cutoff is performed once per block of ten degradation cycles to establish that block's ground-truth SOH via measured Ah capacity. One full charge~$\rightarrow$~SOH discharge~$\rightarrow$~SoC sweep~$\rightarrow$~ten degradation cycles sequence constitutes one block; the full campaign for the fresh unit comprises 20 such blocks (200 degradation cycles total), giving a wide, densely sampled range of ground-truth SOH values against which the sweep's feature behavior can be examined.

\subsection{Feature Extraction and Analytics Pipeline}
\label{sec:features}

A common analytics pipeline extracts a set of SOH-candidate features from every crank/pulse event and every SOH-discharge file. For crank/pulse events, features are computed from short windows immediately before and during the event: a pre-event rest-voltage window gives the open-circuit voltage $V_{OCV}$ (and, via the lookup table of Section~\ref{sec:testdays}, an SoC estimate), a short window at the start of the event gives the loaded voltage and current, and DC internal resistance is estimated as
\[
\mathrm{DCIR} = \frac{|V_{OCV} - V_{\text{event}}|}{|I_{\text{event}}|} \times 1000 \quad [\mathrm{m}\Omega].
\]
Additional per-event features include the minimum (most-sagged) voltage reached during the event, the voltage in a short window immediately after the event ends (capturing kinetic/diffusion recovery), and, for charge-type events, the rate of voltage rise during the event (charge acceptance) --- a feature that empirically proved to be one of the earliest and most sensitive indicators of degradation for this chemistry, since a healthy cell accepts charge with a slowly rising voltage while a degraded cell's voltage rises noticeably faster under the same current. Charge and discharge throughput (Ah) are integrated from the current signal over each event. For the event-scripted test-day profile of Section~\ref{sec:testdays}, the two crank events per run are additionally distinguished by their chronological position in the fixed event script (first~=~``cold'', second~=~``hot'') rather than by a fixed current or voltage threshold, since real logged crank magnitudes did not reliably separate the two conditions by a fixed threshold; the same relative-rather-than-absolute principle is used to flag whether a run was cut short by hitting the discharge cutoff, by comparing that run's event count against the typical count for the same battery rather than a fixed expected count. \todo{decide, and state explicitly, which of these features are computed identically on the field side (Section~\ref{sec:fielddata}) versus which are lab-only because they require a signal (e.g. a dedicated current sensor) that the field data does not have.}

Ground-truth SOH per block is defined as measured C/5 discharge capacity (via current integration to the 10.8~V cutoff) relative to a reference baseline capacity, i.e. $\mathrm{SOH} = Q_{\mathrm{C/5}} / Q_{\mathrm{baseline}} \times 100\%$. \todo{state which baseline is used in the final Results (first-valid-file capacity vs.\ nameplate 18~Ah) since this rescales all reported SOH values.} One data-quality issue is worth noting explicitly because it affects this ground truth: a logging-rate regression on the data-acquisition side, during part of the campaign, caused some discharge logs to be recorded with irregular, occasionally large gaps between samples; a naive current-integration estimate of Ah silently \emph{undercounts} capacity once the gap between samples exceeds a few seconds, because it is implicitly assuming a shorter, evenly-sampled duration than actually elapsed. This was corrected for genuinely constant-current segments (the C/5 SOH discharge and the C/2 degradation-cycle discharges) by substituting duration~$\times$~mean current whenever the sampling gap exceeds a coarseness threshold; the corresponding CC/CV charge phases, whose current is not constant, cannot be corrected this way and are instead flagged as unreliable rather than silently misreported. \todo{confirm whether any block used in the final Results was affected by this regression, and if so, note that the correction has been applied.}

\subsection{Field Data Acquisition}
\label{sec:fielddata}

To evaluate whether the laboratory-derived features generalize beyond the bench, per-start-event data is additionally available from instrumentation on operational heavy-duty vehicles, covering a multi-battery 24~V starter battery pack (multiple 12~V units in a series/parallel configuration). \todo{Confirm and state explicitly here whether this per-start data is read directly off the vehicle's own CAN bus, or from separate data-logging/DAQ hardware fitted to the pack for this purpose (or both) --- this affects how the paper should describe field data acquisition and what can be claimed about deployability using only stock vehicle signals.} For each recorded engine start, the available field feature set includes: pre-crank (rest) voltage, minimum voltage reached during the crank, the percentage voltage drop between the two, an SoC estimate derived from pre-crank voltage, ambient/battery temperature at the time of the start, an estimated internal resistance (from the voltage drop and an estimated crank current), a post-crank recovery-time feature, and --- because the pack is multi-battery --- a measure of voltage imbalance across the battery groups making up the pack. Not every battery group in the pack has its own direct voltage sensor; some group voltages are estimated rather than directly measured, which is a real difference from the lab side (where every unit under test has its own dedicated, directly-wired sensor) and should be treated as a limitation on field feature fidelity rather than ignored. \todo{state the number of vehicles and the number/date range of start events actually used in the field dataset, and whether all vehicles carry the same pack configuration.}
