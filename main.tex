\begin{abstract}
Heavy-duty diesel vehicles depend on starter batteries capable of sustained high-current cranking, and their gradual degradation is difficult to detect before a failure occurs, often away from base where replacement is difficult. This paper develops candidate state-of-health (SOH) indicating features from a controlled laboratory aging campaign --- a lead-acid/VRLA starter battery cycled through 200 degradation cycles across a range of states of charge (SoC), covering measured capacity from 18.16~Ah down to 0.94~Ah --- and evaluates them against field data from 41 real engine-start events on an operational multi-battery starter pack. \todo{finalize once the field-data-source \todo{} in Section~\ref{sec:fielddata} is confirmed and the abstract's deployability language is checked against it.} We find that the features most strongly correlated with SOH in isolation, pre-crank and rest voltage, are substantially confounded with SoC itself and are not reliable degradation indicators once this is accounted for; crank-event internal-resistance-type features remain predictive after controlling for SoC and are corroborated, at the extreme end of degradation, by a two-order-of-magnitude DCIR increase observed on a second, independently aged unit. Field data shows a physically plausible spread in the analogous internal-resistance feature, consistent in kind with the laboratory finding, though --- given a different battery product in the field, separately implemented feature-extraction pipelines, and the absence of a field ground-truth SOH label --- this is evidence about which feature \emph{family} transfers rather than a validated quantitative lab-to-field mapping. We argue that SoC-independence should be an explicit, tested criterion for any lab-derived SOH feature before it is proposed for field deployment.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

Heavy-duty diesel vehicles rely on a starter battery capable of delivering several hundred amperes for a short but critical interval each time the engine is started. Compared to light-duty passenger vehicles, these platforms typically combine larger-displacement, higher-compression diesel engines with harsher duty cycles: long idle periods interspersed with mission-critical starts, wide ambient temperature ranges, and vibration and shock loads that accelerate mechanical wear inside the battery. A failed or marginal start is not merely an inconvenience in this context --- for fleet operators, emergency services, and other applications where the vehicle must be available on demand, an unexpected failure to crank can take an entire asset out of service at the worst possible moment. \todo{if the paper is meant to stay anchored to the armoured-vehicle motivating case in the introduction, add one sentence here noting that armoured/military heavy-duty vehicles are a representative and demanding example of this vehicle class, without going into vehicle-specific detail} The consequences of an undetected failure are more severe still when the vehicle is deployed away from base --- on a mission, at a remote worksite, or otherwise out in the field --- where a spare battery, a replacement, or even diagnostic equipment may not be readily available; a failure that would be a minor, scheduled task in a depot becomes an operational disruption in the field. This asymmetry --- low cost of replacing a battery proactively versus high cost of a failure at the wrong time and place --- is precisely what makes early, reliable SOH estimation valuable for this vehicle class.

A further practical complication, and one that shapes the methodology of this paper, is the absence of ground-truth SOH labels for batteries in field operation. Laboratory aging campaigns can define SOH unambiguously because the battery's full history and a reference measurement (e.g., a capacity or CCA test) are available on demand. In the field, however, batteries are not routinely pulled from vehicles and capacity-tested; the only readily available signal is whatever can be observed non-invasively via the vehicle's own instrumentation, primarily its CAN bus. Consequently, this paper's field dataset has no directly measured SOH label to regress against, and the lab-to-field comparison in Section~\ref{sec:validation} is necessarily framed in terms of whether \emph{feature behavior} (trends, distributions, SoC-dependence) is consistent between the lab and the field, rather than in terms of absolute field SOH accuracy. No proxy label (e.g. workshop replacement records or CCA test logs) is currently available for the field vehicles either, so this limitation is restated explicitly in Section~\ref{sec:discussion} and should inform how the Results in Section~\ref{sec:results} are interpreted: agreement between lab and field should be read as evidence of consistent \emph{feature behavior}, not as validated field SOH accuracy.

State-of-health (SOH) estimation aims to quantify the remaining capability of a battery --- typically expressed relative to its capacity or cranking-current capability when new --- so that a degrading battery can be flagged and replaced before it fails to start the vehicle. For starter batteries specifically, SOH is commonly inferred from the voltage response during a cranking event: the depth of the voltage dip when the starter motor draws current, the shape of the voltage recovery afterward, and derived quantities such as internal resistance all change measurably as the battery ages~\cite{kerleyAutomotiveLeadacidBattery2015,stepresponse_soh}. These cranking-voltage-based methods are attractive because they require no additional current sensor and can, in principle, be implemented using signals already available on the vehicle's data bus.

However, most published SOH estimation methods for lead-acid and AGM starter batteries are developed and validated under controlled laboratory conditions: a single battery (or small batch) is aged on a bench under a scripted, repeatable protocol, and the resulting features are correlated against a reference SOH measurement obtained under the same controlled conditions~\cite{lead_acid_impedance_aging,eis_soh_timedep,ruetschi_aging}. Laboratory aging protocols are valuable because they allow SOH to be tracked against a trustworthy ground truth and because operating conditions --- temperature, load profile, SoC at the time of cranking --- can be swept systematically. What they cannot fully capture is the variability of real-world use: field batteries experience unscripted combinations of temperature, partial charge history, and load that are difficult to reproduce exactly on a bench. Recent work on electric-vehicle traction batteries has shown that this laboratory-to-field gap is real and non-trivial: models and features that perform well on laboratory cycling data can degrade substantially when applied to field data, because field data is noisier, less regularly sampled, and subject to a much wider range of operating conditions than laboratory data~\cite{labfield_gap,transferable_capacity_estimation,multimodal_ev_field}. To date, this transferability question has been studied primarily for lithium-ion traction batteries in electric vehicles. Whether the same gap exists --- and how large it is --- for lead-acid/AGM \emph{starter} batteries under high-current cranking loads in heavy-duty diesel vehicles is, to the best of our knowledge, not well documented in the literature.

This paper addresses that gap directly. We combine two complementary data sources:

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Cranking-Voltage and Impedance-Based SOH Estimation}

A substantial body of work estimates lead-acid/AGM battery SOH from the voltage signature recorded during an engine-cranking event; see~\cite{jiangReviewStateHealth2022} for a review specific to lead-acid SOH estimation methods, and~\cite{nouraReviewBatteryState2020,kimBatteryStateHealth2025} for reviews of the broader SOH estimation landscape. Kerley, Hyun, and Ha propose a SOH monitoring system for lead-acid car batteries based on the cranking voltage waveform, extracting critical voltage points from the response and relating them to battery health without requiring a dedicated current sensor~\cite{kerleyAutomotiveLeadacidBattery2015}. A parity-relation-based approach has also been proposed for automotive battery SOH monitoring, comparing the observed charging excitation voltage against a model-predicted value to flag degradation~\cite{zhangAutomotiveBatteryStateofhealth2012}. A complementary line of work uses a step-response model together with in-situ vehicle data to estimate SOH from the battery's dynamic response to a load step, which is conceptually close to a cranking event but framed in terms of an identified equivalent-circuit model rather than raw voltage features~\cite{stepresponse_soh}; dynamic lead-acid battery models built directly from manufacturer discharge-curve data offer a related, lower-effort route to a similar equivalent-circuit representation~\cite{medoraDynamicBatteryModeling2005}.

Impedance-based methods form a second major family. AC impedance (commonly measured near 1~kHz) increases as a lead-acid battery ages due to sulfation, active-material degradation, and grid corrosion, and has been used both as a direct SOH indicator and as a way to validate voltage- or current-based features against an independent physical measurement~\cite{lead_acid_impedance_aging,eis_soh_timedep,tairovImpedanceMeasurementsBattery2011}. For lead-acid chemistries specifically, EIS has also been used to assess SOH in the context of second-life applications~\cite{mohsinNewLeadacidBattery2022}. Some laboratory protocols combine impedance measurements taken at two distinct states of charge (e.g., full charge and 75\% SoC) to predict remaining battery lifetime with good accuracy~\cite{eis_soh_timedep}, which is directly relevant to the SoC-sweeping design used in this paper's laboratory campaign (Section~\ref{sec:method}).

\subsection{Lead-Acid Battery Aging Mechanisms and SoC Dependence}

The dominant aging mechanisms of lead-acid batteries --- grid and post corrosion, degradation and loss of adherence of the positive active mass, irreversible sulfation, internal short circuits, and water loss --- are well characterized in the foundational review by Ruetschi~\cite{ruetschi_aging}. Of particular relevance to this work, several of these mechanisms are strongly state-of-charge dependent: increasing depth-of-discharge during cycling accelerates positive active-mass degradation, and cumulative time spent at low SoC (commonly cited around SoC~$<$~35\%) is itself an aging stress factor, independent of the number of cycles performed~\cite{ruetschi_aging,lead_acid_impedance_aging}. This motivates deliberately sampling multiple SoC operating points --- rather than aging the battery at a single fixed SoC --- when designing a laboratory campaign intended to produce features that remain valid across the SoC range a starter battery actually experiences in the field. Beyond electrochemical stressors, heavy-duty and off-road duty cycles also subject the battery to sustained vibration and shock loading, separately shown to accelerate lead-acid degradation through plate and grid damage~\cite{heVibrationTestMethods2014} --- a further reason to age the battery under a realistic, vehicle-representative test-day protocol rather than a purely electrical bench cycle. Hybrid lead-acid/lithium-ion energy-storage architectures, in which a smaller lithium-ion buffer absorbs high-current transients to reduce cycling stress on the lead-acid unit, have also been proposed for transport applications~\cite{zauReviewBatteryManagement2020} and illustrate one mitigation strategy for the aging mechanisms considered here.

\subsection{Field-Data-Driven and Machine-Learning SOH Estimation}

Outside the lead-acid/starter-battery domain, a large and fast-growing literature addresses SOH estimation for lithium-ion traction batteries using field data collected from electric vehicles, typically via onboard telematics or CAN-bus logging. Health indicators are extracted from charging-phase voltage/current/temperature time series and fed to models ranging from Gaussian process regression to CNNs, RNNs, and LSTMs~\cite{multimodal_ev_field,domain_ml_soh}. Recent studies report that models trained on a single fleet or dataset generalize poorly to other vehicles or fleets, motivating transfer-learning and domain-adaptation approaches that explicitly try to bridge differences between training and deployment distributions~\cite{robust_soh_buses,transferable_capacity_estimation}.

Most relevant to the present paper is a recent and still-limited strand of work that studies the laboratory-to-field gap explicitly, rather than working with laboratory or field data in isolation. This work argues that laboratory cycling protocols use predefined, temperature-controlled conditions that do not reflect the partial charging/discharging, variable load, and calendar-aging effects seen in real-world operation, and that SOH algorithms tuned on laboratory data can consequently underperform when applied to field data~\cite{labfield_gap,transferable_capacity_estimation}. These studies are, however, concentrated on lithium-ion EV traction batteries. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has directly evaluated whether cranking-based SOH features derived from a controlled laboratory aging campaign transfer to field CAN-bus data recorded on high-current starter batteries in heavy-duty diesel vehicles. This is the gap addressed in the present paper.

\subsection{Battery Monitoring in Heavy-Duty and Military Vehicle Contexts}

Reliability of the energy supply system, including the starter battery, is also studied directly in the military vehicle literature: Földesi et al.\ propose a hierarchical diagnostics and risk-assessment procedure for military vehicle batteries based on rapid pre-deployment testing, motivated by the fact that an unexpected power deficit is not only costly but can endanger personnel~\cite{foldesiHierarchicalDiagnosticsRisk2022}. This work targets pre-deployment risk scoring rather than a lab-to-field feature-transferability study, and does not address high-current starter batteries specifically, but it confirms that battery-driven mission risk is an established concern in this vehicle class and that rapid, field-deployable diagnostics (as opposed to full laboratory characterization) are the practical constraint operators work under. More broadly, predictive maintenance of heavy-duty vehicle components is an active research area in its own right; recent component-level prognostics work on heavy-duty trucks~\cite{dimidovEmpiricalStudyPredictive2025} and general treatments of the practical challenges of deploying predictive maintenance in the field~\cite{tingaPracticalApplicationPredictive2025} both underscore that translating lab- or fleet-analytics-derived indicators into reliable in-service monitoring remains difficult even outside the battery domain specifically.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}
% ============================================================

The methodology consists of two stages that share a common feature-extraction pipeline: (1) a controlled laboratory aging campaign that induces realistic degradation in a representative battery and produces a labeled feature/SOH dataset, and (2) a field-validation stage in which the same features are extracted from CAN-bus data recorded on operational heavy-duty diesel vehicles and compared against the laboratory findings. \subsection{Battery Under Test}
\label{sec:battery}

The laboratory campaign uses 12~V, 18~Ah valve-regulated lead-acid (VRLA/AGM) batteries (six 2~V cells in series) of the same nominal capacity class as starter batteries used in the target heavy-duty vehicle class. Per the manufacturer datasheet, the unit under test is rated for 15.3~Ah at the C/5 rate to a 1.75~V/cell cutoff, has a new-condition AC internal resistance of approximately 16~m$\Omega$, can sustain 270~A discharge for 5~s, and is specified for cycle-use charging at 14.4--15.0~V (max. 5.4~A) with an end-of-discharge cutoff of 10.8~V (1.8~V/cell) at the standard rate. Two physical units are tracked through the campaign under distinct identifiers, denoted here Unit~A (the already-aged unit: it entered the campaign substantially degraded, delivering only $\sim$8~Ah of its 18~Ah nameplate capacity on an early full discharge, and has since been retired) and Unit~B (a fresh unit whose full degradation history from new is captured by the campaign). \textbf{The two units are never pooled}: they are tracked under distinct identifiers throughout the analytics pipeline and reported separately in Section~\ref{sec:results}, since pooling would mix two different starting conditions and degradation states into a single, physically meaningless trend. Unit~B's campaign is the primary source of the SoC-sweep and degradation-trend results in Section~\ref{sec:results}, since it captures the full range from new to heavily degraded (see Section~\ref{sec:testdays}); Unit~A's already-aged, shorter dataset serves as a consistency check at the low-SOH end and separately illustrates the DCIR/impedance blow-up characteristic of a heavily sulfated cell (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\textbf{An important limitation for the lab-to-field comparison of Section~\ref{sec:validation}, not previously flagged}: the field battery pack described in Section~\ref{sec:fielddata} is a different make and model (a 24~V, multi-unit pack built from larger nominal-capacity cells) than the 12~V/18~Ah unit under test here. The lab-to-field comparison in this paper is therefore \emph{cross-model} transferability --- whether the same feature \emph{types} (e.g. crank-response internal resistance) behave consistently across a different lead-acid/VRLA product, not same-battery or same-pack-architecture validation. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} returns to how this constrains the strength of the paper's transferability claim. Using a production battery of the vehicle's own capacity/voltage class, rather than a generic reference cell, is intended to keep the cranking-current demand, internal resistance, and aging behavior representative of what is actually observed on the vehicle's own instrumentation during field validation (Section~\ref{sec:fielddata}).

\subsection{Laboratory Test Bench}
\label{sec:testbench}

The test bench is built around a Raspberry Pi running a Python-based orchestration layer that sequences charge, discharge, degradation-cycling, and test-day-profile phases and exposes a live web dashboard for monitoring and control. Electrical loading is applied via a bidirectional programmable power supply, addressed over a TCP/SCPI interface, capable of sourcing (charging) or sinking (discharging) up to 90~A / 15~V / 1500~W; this replaces a fixed resistive load or relay-switched load bank with a supply that can execute an arbitrary current or voltage profile in either polarity, which is what allows the event-scripted test-day profile of Section~\ref{sec:testdays} to be replayed directly rather than approximated. Voltage and current are logged by the power supply itself at 1~Hz during charge/discharge/degradation phases and at 20~Hz during test-day-profile replay (the higher rate being needed to resolve the sub-second structure of a simulated cranking pulse). An independent sensor board (microcontroller-based, serial link to the Pi) samples battery terminal voltage (via a voltage divider) and current (via a Hall-effect current sensor) at 20~Hz, together with ambient temperature and humidity at 1~Hz, providing a cross-check against the power supply's own measurement channel. All logs are timestamped in a common per-battery, per-run naming scheme and written to persistent storage for offline processing by the analytics pipeline described in Section~\ref{sec:features}.

\subsection{Design of the SoC-Sweeping Test-Day Protocol}
\label{sec:testdays}

Rather than aging the battery under a single fixed condition, the campaign repeatedly executes a realistic test-day load profile at up to ten different states of charge per degradation block, sweeping from fully charged down toward the discharge cutoff. This design is motivated by the SoC-dependent aging mechanisms discussed in Section~\ref{sec:related}: because both the electrochemical aging rate and the electrical behavior of the battery during a cranking event depend on SoC, features that are only characterized at a single SoC risk failing to generalize across the SoC range the battery actually experiences in the field.

A key design decision is how SoC is set and labeled. A fixed Ah step-down (e.g., discharge a constant 10\% of nameplate capacity between sweep points) becomes a meaningless SoC label as the battery degrades, since a fixed Ah quantity is a shrinking, then eventually undefined, fraction of a capacity that is itself falling. Instead, each sweep point is defined and verified by open-circuit voltage (OCV) after a rest period, using a piecewise-linear OCV--SoC lookup table calibrated for this VRLA/AGM chemistry at 25\textdegree{}C (Table~\ref{tab:testdays}). This choice is deliberate: it matches the only SoC-related quantity that is realistically observable on the vehicle at rest (terminal voltage), so the lab-side SoC label and the field-side SoC proxy are defined the same way, rather than the lab relying on a Coulomb-counted SoC that has no field equivalent. At each sweep point, the battery is stepped down using a rest-first loop --- disable load, rest, measure OCV, and only discharge further if OCV is still above the next target --- so that a battery that already reached a target point (e.g., as a side effect of the previous test-day profile) is not discharged unnecessarily.

At each sweep point, a single ``realistic test day'' is executed as an explicit, named event script rather than an unstructured load: an open-circuit rest window, a small wake-up/parasitic load, a glow-plug-like preheat load, a first (``cold'') high-current crank pulse, a recovery rest, a second glow-plug-like load, a second (``hot'') crank pulse, a further recovery rest, and finally an alternator-like charge phase followed by a variable driving/auxiliary-load phase. Including two crank events per test day, rather than one, allows the same battery's cranking signature to be compared across a cold-start-like and a warm-restart-like condition at the same SoC, in addition to across SoC. \todo{state the actual crank-pulse current/duration values used, and the wake-up/glow-plug/driving-load current levels, from the profile CSV, so a reader can judge how demanding the simulated crank is relative to the datasheet's 270~A/5~s rating.} Ten deep degradation cycles (discharge and CC/CV recharge at the C/2 rate) are interleaved between successive sweeps to progressively age the battery, and a full C/5-rate discharge to the 10.8~V cutoff is performed once per block of ten degradation cycles to establish that block's ground-truth SOH via measured Ah capacity. One full charge~$\rightarrow$~SOH discharge~$\rightarrow$~SoC sweep~$\rightarrow$~ten degradation cycles sequence constitutes one block; the full campaign for the fresh unit comprises 20 such blocks (200 degradation cycles total), giving a wide, densely sampled range of ground-truth SOH values against which the sweep's feature behavior can be examined.

\subsection{Feature Extraction and Analytics Pipeline}
\label{sec:features}

A common analytics pipeline extracts a set of SOH-candidate features from every crank/pulse event and every SOH-discharge file. For crank/pulse events, features are computed from short windows immediately before and during the event: a pre-event rest-voltage window gives the open-circuit voltage $V_{OCV}$ (and, via the lookup table of Section~\ref{sec:testdays}, an SoC estimate), a short window at the start of the event gives the loaded voltage and current, and DC internal resistance is estimated as
\[
\mathrm{DCIR} = \frac{|V_{OCV} - V_{\text{event}}|}{|I_{\text{event}}|} \times 1000 \quad [\mathrm{m}\Omega].
\]
Additional per-event features include the minimum (most-sagged) voltage reached during the event, the voltage in a short window immediately after the event ends (capturing kinetic/diffusion recovery), and, for charge-type events, the rate of voltage rise during the event (charge acceptance) --- a feature that empirically proved to be one of the earliest and most sensitive indicators of degradation for this chemistry, since a healthy cell accepts charge with a slowly rising voltage while a degraded cell's voltage rises noticeably faster under the same current. Charge and discharge throughput (Ah) are integrated from the current signal over each event. For the event-scripted test-day profile of Section~\ref{sec:testdays}, the two crank events per run are additionally distinguished by their chronological position in the fixed event script (first~=~``cold'', second~=~``hot'') rather than by a fixed current or voltage threshold, since real logged crank magnitudes did not reliably separate the two conditions by a fixed threshold; the same relative-rather-than-absolute principle is used to flag whether a run was cut short by hitting the discharge cutoff, by comparing that run's event count against the typical count for the same battery rather than a fixed expected count. Of this feature set, the ones with a direct field-side counterpart (Section~\ref{sec:fielddata}) are the crank-event internal-resistance features, minimum/pre-event voltage, and post-event recovery; features that depend on the full event script (e.g. the alternator-charge and driving/auxiliary-load features) or on a dedicated current sensor beyond what a crank event itself provides are lab-only for now.

Because a feature's raw correlation with SOH can be misleading when the feature is also strongly correlated with SoC (a feature that is really just tracking SoC will still correlate with SOH across a SoC sweep, since both drift together over the campaign, without genuinely reflecting degradation), every candidate feature is additionally checked against SoC itself. A feature is treated as a genuine, SoC-robust SOH indicator only if it correlates with measured SOH \emph{after} accounting for its correlation with SoC, rather than on raw correlation alone; Section~\ref{sec:results} reports both figures for the lab's top candidate features.

Ground-truth SOH per block is defined as measured C/5 discharge capacity (via current integration to the 10.8~V cutoff) relative to the manufacturer's 18~Ah nameplate capacity, i.e. $\mathrm{SOH} = Q_{\mathrm{C/5}} / 18~\mathrm{Ah} \times 100\%$; this nameplate baseline is used rather than a first-valid-file baseline so that SOH values are directly comparable across Unit~A and Unit~B despite their different starting condition. One data-quality issue is worth noting explicitly because it affects this ground truth: a logging-rate regression on the data-acquisition side, occurring on Unit~B during blocks 4--5 of its 20-block campaign, caused some discharge logs to be recorded with irregular, occasionally large gaps between samples; a naive current-integration estimate of Ah silently \emph{undercounts} capacity once the gap between samples exceeds a few seconds, because it is implicitly assuming a shorter, evenly-sampled duration than actually elapsed. This was corrected for genuinely constant-current segments (the C/5 SOH discharge and the C/2 degradation-cycle discharges) by substituting duration~$\times$~mean current whenever the sampling gap exceeds a coarseness threshold; the corresponding CC/CV charge phases, whose current is not constant, cannot be corrected this way and are instead flagged as unreliable rather than silently misreported. All SOH and feature values reported in Section~\ref{sec:results} for Unit~B are taken after this correction (and after two further, unrelated analysis-code fixes to the degradation-cycle completeness check and a hardcoded low-capacity floor that had been silently dropping genuine late-campaign readings); Section~\ref{sec:discussion} nonetheless treats the fact that these issues occurred at all as a limitation worth stating plainly, since it means the raw logging pipeline was not fully reliable throughout and later-discovered issues of the same kind cannot be ruled out.

This matters for the paper's claims: a feature set that only works via added telematics hardware is a weaker deployability claim than one that works from signals already present on a stock vehicle CAN bus, and the paper's current framing (Introduction, Related Work) assumes the latter throughout --- that framing should be revisited once this is confirmed.} For each recorded engine start, the available field feature set includes: pre-crank (rest) voltage, minimum voltage reached during the crank, the percentage voltage drop between the two, an SoC estimate derived from pre-crank voltage, ambient/battery temperature at the time of the start, an estimated internal resistance (from the voltage drop and an estimated crank current), a post-crank recovery-time feature, and --- because the pack is multi-battery --- a measure of voltage imbalance across the four battery groups making up the pack. Not every battery group in the pack has its own direct voltage sensor: two of the four groups are directly measured (via a center-tap sensor) and two are estimated rather than directly measured, which is a real difference from the lab side (where the single unit under test has its own dedicated, directly-wired sensor) and should be treated as a limitation on field feature fidelity rather than ignored. One further data-quality note on the estimated channels: across all 41 recorded starts, the estimated internal-resistance values for one of the two estimated groups are numerically identical, sample for sample, to those of one of the two directly-measured groups, while the other estimated group's values are not identical to its corresponding measured group. This is consistent with (at least) one of the two estimation channels not being independent of its measured counterpart --- e.g., derived from it algebraically rather than from an independent estimate --- and is treated in Section~\ref{sec:results} as a reason to report pack-level (rather than fine-grained per-group) field results as primary, with group-level imbalance reported only as a coarser, secondary signal.

\subsection{Lab-to-Field Validation Procedure}
\label{sec:validation}

The validation procedure compares, feature by feature, the lab-side crank/pulse features of Section~\ref{sec:features} against their field-side counterparts from Section~\ref{sec:fielddata} --- e.g., lab DCIR/crank-apparent internal resistance against field estimated internal resistance, lab post-event recovery voltage against field recovery time, lab minimum event voltage against field minimum crank voltage and percentage voltage drop. \textbf{This is a comparison of conceptually paired, analogously-defined features, not a single shared feature-extraction codebase applied unmodified to both datasets}: the lab and field features are currently computed by two separate analytics pipelines (one per data source), and while the feature \emph{definitions} are designed to match as closely as the two signal sets allow, they have not been verified to produce numerically identical output given identical input. A shared implementation is future work (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}); the present comparison should be read as evaluating whether the same physical quantities behave consistently across lab and field, not as a strict software-level reproducibility check.

Because the field dataset carries no ground-truth SOH label (Section~\ref{sec:intro}), the comparison is framed as feature-behavior consistency rather than SOH-accuracy validation, along two of the three axes originally considered: (a) whether a feature's relationship to lab-aged SOH (across Unit~B's 20 blocks) is directionally consistent with that feature's spread across the field's 41 starts --- performed in Section~\ref{sec:results} for internal-resistance-type features; (b) whether a feature's SoC-dependence in the lab sweep is reproduced in field starts at a similar estimated SoC --- performed by explicitly separating, on the lab side, features whose apparent SOH-predictive power turns out to be mostly a SoC proxy from those that remain predictive after controlling for SoC (Section~\ref{sec:features}), and checking whether the latter group is the group that also varies sensibly with field SoC. The third axis --- (c) whether the lab's cold-vs-hot crank distinction has an observable field analogue such as ambient temperature at start --- is \textbf{not} performed in this draft: the field feature set (Section~\ref{sec:fielddata}) does not currently distinguish repeated starts within a short window the way the lab's scripted cold/hot pair does, and is left as future work (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). The outcome of this procedure is a per-feature assessment of laboratory-to-field transferability, reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Laboratory Results}
\label{sec:results-lab}

\textbf{Unit~B (fresh unit, full campaign).} The 20-block campaign completed with measured C/5 capacity declining from 18.16~Ah (block~1, 100.9\% of the 18~Ah nameplate) to 0.94~Ah (block~20, 5.2\% of nameplate) --- a monotonic decline over 200 degradation cycles, with one minor non-monotonic reading at block~6 (13.97~Ah, above block~5's 12.12~Ah), consistent with ordinary measurement noise rather than a data-quality issue. This gives a densely sampled, wide-range SOH trajectory against which candidate features can be evaluated.

\todo{add a table here with the top rows of \texttt{soh\_predictors\_this\_next\_block.csv} and \texttt{soc\_robust\_soh\_indicators.csv} (raw $r$ vs.\ SoC-adjusted quality score per feature); summarized in prose only for now.} For each candidate feature, two figures matter: its raw Spearman correlation with next-block measured capacity, and the same correlation after accounting for the feature's own correlation with SoC (Section~\ref{sec:features}). The distinction matters substantially. The raw correlations are strongest for pre-crank/rest-voltage features --- the OCV-window pre-crank voltage ($r=-0.94$ against next-block capacity) and the rest-baseline mean voltage ($r=-0.94$) --- and for hot-crank peak current ($r=0.92$). However, both rest-voltage features are also almost perfectly correlated with the sweep's own SoC value ($r_{\mathrm{SoC}} = 0.98$--$0.99$), meaning most of their apparent predictive power is a SoC proxy rather than a genuine degradation signal: across a SoC sweep repeated at progressively lower average SoC as the battery ages, a purely SoC-tracking feature will still correlate with capacity without reflecting degradation directly. After controlling for this, the leading SoC-robust indicators are the standard deviation of voltage during the driving/auxiliary-load phase ($r_{\mathrm{SoC}}=0.25$, $r_{\text{next-block}}=-0.91$) and the voltage sag during the wake-up load ($r_{\mathrm{SoC}}=0.09$, $r_{\text{next-block}}=-0.66$), followed by the alternator-charge Ah delivered after the cold crank and the apparent internal resistance during the hot crank event --- the latter the single most SoC-independent candidate feature found ($r_{\mathrm{SoC}}=0.04$), though with a more modest raw correlation ($r=-0.35$).

\textbf{Unit~A (already-aged unit).} Across 13 valid C/5 discharges (Dec.\ 2025--June 2026), measured capacity ranged 5.60--14.08~Ah, i.e. 43.7\%--109.9\% relative to its first valid discharge; the 109.9\% reading is higher than the reference discharge it is normalized against, which is inconsistent with monotonic aging and is not explained further here (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). During Unit~A's SoC sweep, DCIR reached up to 1802.8~m$\Omega$ at low SoC --- roughly two orders of magnitude above the 40--130~m$\Omega$ typically seen at higher SoC in the same sweep, and far above the $\sim$16~m$\Omega$ new-condition datasheet value --- illustrating how dramatically internal-resistance-type features blow up for a severely degraded, already-sulfated cell, consistent with Unit~B's finding that internal-resistance features are informative but demonstrating a much larger dynamic range than seen anywhere in Unit~B's own (less extreme, at 5.2\% nameplate) end state.

\subsection{Field Results}
\label{sec:results-field}

The field dataset comprises 41 real engine-start events from a single 24~V, four-unit battery pack. Pack-level summary statistics: mean voltage drop during crank 11.1\% (range 4.8--36.5\%), mean estimated internal resistance 12.4~m$\Omega$ (range 6.5--44.5~m$\Omega$), mean post-crank recovery time 0.99~s (range 0.01--1.51~s), mean pre-crank SoC estimate 67.6\% (range 40--100\%), mean ambient temperature at start 13.7\textdegree{}C (range 2.1--21.2\textdegree{}C). Estimated internal resistance correlates strongly with cross-group voltage imbalance ($r=0.80$) but only weakly with elapsed time across the observed window ($r=0.26$) --- consistent with the field window (several months) not yet being long enough to show an independent long-term aging trend the way Unit~B's 20-block, multi-month lab campaign does against block number. A small number of high-resistance, high-imbalance events (including one at a nominally high estimated SoC, where high resistance is not expected) look more consistent with one degrading battery group within the pack than with pack-wide aging, though this cannot be confirmed without independent per-group ground truth (Section~\ref{sec:fielddata}).

\subsection{Lab-to-Field Comparison}
\label{sec:results-comparison}

Both datasets converge on internal-resistance/crank-response-type features as the more physically meaningful signal family, and on simple rest-voltage as a less trustworthy one: Unit~A's DCIR spans roughly two orders of magnitude between healthy and severely degraded readings, Unit~B's most SoC-robust SOH-predictive feature is a crank-event internal-resistance quantity rather than a rest-voltage quantity, and the field's estimated internal resistance --- the feature most directly analogous to these lab quantities --- shows a physically plausible spread (6.5--44.5~m$\Omega$) across real starts. The lab's central methodological finding, that rest-voltage features are largely SoC proxies once checked against SoC-independence, is also a useful caution for interpreting the field data: the field's own SoC estimate is itself derived from pre-crank voltage, so the same entanglement between voltage, SoC, and apparent degradation signal that was demonstrated explicitly in the lab plausibly also affects field feature interpretation, even though it cannot be independently verified there without a field ground-truth SOH label.

This convergence is at the level of \emph{which feature type is informative}, not a validated quantitative transfer of feature values. As Sections~\ref{sec:battery} and~\ref{sec:validation} state explicitly, the lab unit and the field pack are different battery products, the two feature sets are computed by separate (not shared) analytics pipelines, and the field window does not yet show its own clear aging trend against elapsed time. \textbf{The result of this comparison is therefore: consistent evidence, across two different lead-acid/VRLA products, that crank/pulse-response internal-resistance-type features are a more SOH-relevant signal family than simple rest voltage --- but not yet a validated, quantitative same-battery lab-to-field transfer of a specific feature-to-SOH mapping.} Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses what would be needed to close that gap.

% ============================================================
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================

\textbf{Lab-only feature development risks selecting SoC proxies, not degradation indicators.} The clearest methodological finding of this paper (Section~\ref{sec:results-lab}) is that the features with the strongest raw correlation to measured SOH --- pre-crank and rest-baseline voltage --- are also nearly perfectly correlated with SoC itself, and lose most of their apparent predictive power once SoC is accounted for. Had the SoC sweep not been designed to vary SoC independently of degradation (Section~\ref{sec:testdays}), this confound would not have been detectable at all: a campaign that always measured SOH at a fixed SoC could not distinguish a genuine degradation feature from a SoC proxy, since the two would be perfectly aliased. This is, we believe, a more general caution for lab-based SOH feature development than specific to this battery: any candidate feature should be checked for SoC-independence before being reported as SOH-predictive, and a sweep design like the one used here is one practical way to make that check possible.

\textbf{Practical implication for deployment.} Of the candidate features examined, crank-event internal-resistance-type quantities (available from voltage and current during a start the vehicle is already performing, requiring no additional sensor beyond what a crank event itself provides) are a more defensible basis for on-vehicle SOH monitoring than simple pre-crank or rest voltage, precisely because the latter is shown here to be substantially confounded with SoC. This is consistent with, and adds a concrete SoC-confound demonstration to, the broader cranking-voltage and impedance-based SOH literature reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:related}.

\textbf{Limitations.} Several limit how strongly this paper's results should be read. (1) \emph{Cross-model comparison}: the lab unit and the field pack are different lead-acid/VRLA products (Section~\ref{sec:battery}), so lab-to-field consistency is evidence at the level of feature \emph{type}, not a validated transfer of a specific feature-to-SOH mapping. (2) \emph{Separate pipelines}: lab and field features are computed by independently implemented analytics code with matched definitions but unverified numerical equivalence (Section~\ref{sec:validation}). (3) \emph{Uncertain field data provenance}: whether the field data originates from the vehicle's stock CAN bus or from added fleet-management/telematics hardware is not yet confirmed (Section~\ref{sec:fielddata}), which directly affects how strong a deployability claim the paper can make. (4) \emph{Field data scope}: 41 starts from a single pack over several months is not a fleet and is not yet long enough to show its own independent aging trend (Section~\ref{sec:results-field}). (5) \emph{No field ground-truth SOH}, as established in Section~\ref{sec:intro}; all field-side conclusions are behavioral/qualitative. (6) \emph{Known lab data-quality incidents}: a Pi-side logging-rate regression during Unit~B's blocks 4--5, and two since-fixed analysis-code bugs (a completeness check using a global rather than per-block median, and a hardcoded capacity floor that dropped genuine late-campaign low readings), were all corrected before the results reported here, but their occurrence indicates the logging/analysis pipeline was not fully reliable throughout, and similar undetected issues cannot be ruled out. (7) \emph{Field per-group granularity is suspect}: one of the field pack's two estimated (non-directly-sensed) battery groups produces internal-resistance values numerically identical to a directly-measured group across all 41 starts, suggesting that estimation channel may not be independent, so fine-grained per-group field comparisons should be treated cautiously (Section~\ref{sec:fielddata}). (8) Unit~A shows one capacity reading (109.9\% relative to its own first-valid baseline) inconsistent with monotonic aging, not explained further here.

\textbf{Future work.} The most direct way to close the gap between the qualitative convergence found here and a validated quantitative transfer would be: a shared, single feature-extraction implementation applied to both lab and field signals; confirmation of the field data's true provenance (CAN bus vs.\ telematics/DAQ) and, if the latter, an assessment of whether the same features remain extractable from stock CAN signals alone; an expanded field dataset covering more vehicles/packs and a longer observation window, ideally with a weak SOH proxy such as workshop battery-replacement records; explicit detection of closely repeated field starts to test for a cold/hot-equivalent effect; and, most directly addressing the cross-model limitation, testing at least one lab unit of the same make/model as the field pack.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

This paper set out to test whether SOH-indicating features identified in a controlled laboratory aging campaign transfer to field conditions for high-current starter batteries in heavy-duty vehicles, given that field batteries typically have no ground-truth SOH label to validate against. A 20-block laboratory campaign on a fresh 12~V/18~Ah VRLA/AGM unit, spanning a full degradation trajectory from 18.16~Ah to 0.94~Ah of measured C/5 capacity, showed that the features most strongly correlated with SOH on their own --- pre-crank and rest voltage --- are largely SoC proxies rather than genuine degradation indicators, while crank-event internal-resistance-type features remain predictive after accounting for SoC and are therefore the more trustworthy candidates. A second, already-aged lab unit corroborated the internal-resistance finding at the extreme end of degradation, where DCIR rose roughly two orders of magnitude above its healthy-condition value. Field data from 41 real engine starts on an operational multi-battery pack showed a physically plausible spread in the field-side analogue of this same internal-resistance feature, consistent in \emph{kind} with the lab finding. However, because the field pack is a different battery product from the lab unit, the two feature sets are computed by separate analytics pipelines, and no field ground-truth SOH exists, this convergence should be read as consistent evidence about which feature \emph{family} matters --- not as a validated, quantitative lab-to-field transfer of a specific feature-to-SOH relationship. The practical takeaway for SOH monitoring of high-current starter batteries in heavy-duty diesel vehicles is twofold: prioritize crank/pulse-response internal-resistance-type features over simple rest-voltage features, and explicitly test any candidate feature for SoC-independence, using a SoC-varying protocol like the one presented here, before treating it as a genuine SOH indicator.
